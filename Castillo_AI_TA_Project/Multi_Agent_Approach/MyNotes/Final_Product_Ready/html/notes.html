1 Scattering by a central potential

For a particle of mass m, the incident energy, the incident velocity and the angular momentum are

E = m 2 v02, (1) v0 = 2E∕m, (2) ℓ = smv0 = s2𝑚𝐸, (3)

where s is the impact parameter, so that

s = ℓ 2𝑚𝐸. (4)

The geometry these quantities describe is set out in Fig. 1.

The volume element and the solid angle are

d3r = r2drdφsin⁡ ⁡𝜃d𝜃⏟ = dΩ,dΩ = 2πsin⁡ ⁡𝜃d𝜃, (5)

the azimuthal angle φ integrating to 2π because it is irrelevant by symmetry. The quantity to be computed is s(𝜃,E).

1.1 Incident intensity and differential cross section

The incident intensity and the differential cross section are defined as ratios of counts:

I = number of particles incident per unit time incident beam cross-sectional area , (6)

σ(Ω)dΩ = number of particles scattered into dΩ per unit time incident intensity . (7)

For a central potential σ depends on Ω only through 𝜃, so σ(Ω) and σ(𝜃) denote the same function throughout.

Particles crossing the incident ring between s and s + ds are exactly those emerging with scattering angle between 𝜃 and 𝜃 + d𝜃 (Fig. 1), so the two counts are equal:

I(2𝜋𝑠)|ds| = particles through the ring between s and s + ds = particles emerging between 𝜃 and 𝜃 + d𝜃 = Iσ(𝜃)2πsin⁡𝜃|d𝜃|. (8)

Therefore

σ(𝜃) = s sin⁡𝜃 |ds d𝜃 | (9)

Define also the total cross section

σT = ∫ dΩσ(Ω) (10)

1.2 The deflection angle and the apsidal angle

The two signs of the force give the two geometries of Figs. 2 and 3.

In the repulsive case

π = 𝜃 + 2ψ,𝜃 = π − 2ψ,ψ = π − 𝜃 2 , (11)

1.3 Computing s(𝜃,E): the universal method

The radial energy equation and the angular momentum together reduce the orbit to a quadrature. Writing 𝜃~ for the running orbit angle, to keep it distinct from the scattering angle 𝜃,

E = m 2 ṙ2 + V (r) + ℓ2 2mr2, (14) ṙ = [ 2 m (E − V (r) − ℓ2 2mr2 )] 1∕2,ṙ = dr dt⟹dt = dr ṙ, (15)

and, since ℓ = mr2d𝜃~ dt ,

d𝜃~ dt = ℓ mr2⟹d𝜃~ = ℓ mr2dt = ℓ mr2 dr [ 2 m (E − V (r) − ℓ2 2mr2 )] 1∕2. (16)

where 𝜃~m is the orbit angle at the apsis and rm the corresponding radius.

Eq. (18) then becomes

2 Coulomb scattering

Charges are written in Gaussian units throughout, as in the reference convention, so that the Coulomb force carries no factor of 4π𝜖0. The two like charges of Fig. 4 drive each other apart.

The force and potential are

f = ZZ′e2 r2 ,f = −k r2,V = −k r,k = −ZZ′e2. (22)

For scattering, E > 0, and the orbit is

1 r = C [1 + 𝜀cos⁡(𝜃~ −𝜃~′)],C = 𝑚𝑘 ℓ2 = −m ℓ2ZZ′e2, (23)

with eccentricity

𝜀 = [1 + 2Eℓ2 mk2 ] 1∕2 = [1 + 2Eℓ2 m(ZZ′e2)2 ] 1∕2 = [1 + ( 2𝑠𝐸 ZZ′e2 ) 2] 1∕2 > 1, (24)

the last step following from ℓ = smv0 = s2𝑚𝐸, which gives

2ℓ2E m = s222mE2 m = (2𝑠𝐸)2. (25)

The orbit types this potential admits are listed in Table 1.

There are two situations:

  1. repulsive case, ZZ′ > 0⟹k = −ZZ′e2 < 0;
  2. attractive case, ZZ′ < 0⟹k = −ZZ′e2 > 0 (also gravity, k = Gm1m2).

2.1 Repulsive case

Here k < 0 and C = 𝑚𝑘∕ℓ2 < 0, so, dividing by the negative C and reversing the inequality at each step,

1 r = C [1 + 𝜀cos⁡(𝜃~ −𝜃~′)] > 0 ⟺1 + 𝜀cos⁡(𝜃~ −𝜃~′) < 0 ⟺𝜀cos⁡(𝜃~ −𝜃~′) < −1 ⟺cos⁡(𝜃~ −𝜃~′) < −1 𝜀. (26)

The cosine must be negative and larger in magnitude than 1∕𝜀. In this case 𝜃~ = 𝜃~′ is not a physical point, because cos⁡(𝜃~′−𝜃~′) = cos⁡0 = 1 would give 1 r = C [1 + 𝜀] < 0, which is unphysical. So 𝜃~ = 𝜃~′ lies in the middle of the inaccessible region of the hyperbola, and 𝜃~ = 𝜃~′± π is the periapsis; choosing 𝜃~′ = π gives

cos⁡(𝜃~ −𝜃~′) = cos⁡(𝜃~ − π) = cos⁡𝜃~cos⁡ ⁡π⏟ −1 + sin⁡ ⁡𝜃~sin⁡ ⁡π⏟ 0 = −cos⁡ ⁡𝜃~, (27)

and therefore

1 r = (−𝑚𝑘 ℓ2 ) [−1 − 𝜀cos⁡(𝜃~ −𝜃~′)] = mZZ′e2 ℓ2 [𝜀cos⁡𝜃~ − 1]. (28)

This is a maximum at 𝜃~ = 0, so the minimum radius is

1 rm = mZZ′e2 ℓ2 [𝜀 − 1], (29)

and the angle at the apsidal point is 𝜃~ = 0. From here on the origin of 𝜃~ is the apsidal line, so the apsis is at 𝜃~ = 0. In Eq. (18) the origin was the forward beam axis, with the incoming asymptote at 𝜃~ = π.

What is needed now is ψ = 𝜃~(r →∞) = 𝜃~(1∕r → 0). In the repulsive case 𝜃 = π − 2ψ, so ψ = (π − 𝜃)∕2 and

0 = 𝜀cos⁡ψ − 1⟺1 𝜀 = cos⁡ψ = cos⁡ (π − 𝜃 2 ) = cos⁡ ⁡ π 2⏟ 0 cos⁡ ⁡ 𝜃 2 + sin⁡ ⁡ π 2⏟ 1 sin⁡ ⁡ 𝜃 2 = sin⁡ ⁡ 𝜃 2. (30)

2.2 Attractive case

Here k > 0 and C = 𝑘𝑚∕ℓ2 > 0. Take 𝜃~′ = 0; then 𝜃~ = 𝜃~′ = 0 gives

1 r = C [1 + 𝜀cos⁡(0)] = C [1 + 𝜀], (31)

a maximum at 𝜃~ = 0, so the minimum radius is 1∕rm = C [1 + 𝜀] and the apsidal point is also at 𝜃~ = 0.

Again what is needed is ψ = 𝜃~(r →∞) = 𝜃~(1∕r → 0). In the attractive case 𝜃 = 2ψ − π, so ψ = (π + 𝜃)∕2 and

0 = 1 + 𝜀cos⁡ψ⟺ −1 𝜀 = cos⁡ψ = cos⁡ (π + 𝜃 2 ) = cos⁡ ⁡ π 2⏟ 0 cos⁡ ⁡ 𝜃 2 −sin⁡ ⁡ π 2⏟ 1 sin⁡ ⁡ 𝜃 2 = −sin⁡ ⁡ 𝜃 2. (32)

3.1 Remarks on Eq. (37)

  1. The cross section is the same for the repulsive and attractive cases, since it is proportional to (ZZ′)2.
  2. It diverges like E−2 at low energies.
  3. It diverges like 𝜃−4 (very strongly) for small angles 𝜃.
  4. This classical result is exactly the same as the result obtained in quantum mechanics.

Erratum. (zz′)2 is written in lower case, E−2 and 𝜃−4 as unrendered markup, and the third remark as “VERY STRONG!!!”. The mathematics is set here as mathematics and the casing brought into line with the ZZ′ used throughout.

3.2 Total scattering cross section

The total cross section of Eq. (10) may now be evaluated:

σT = ∫ σ(Ω)dΩ. (38)

In this case σ(𝜃) = const ⋅ 1∕sin⁡4(𝜃∕2), and with

dΩ = 2πsin⁡𝜃d𝜃 = 4πsin⁡ 𝜃 2cos⁡ 𝜃 2d𝜃, (39)

σT = 4π 4 (ZZ′e2 2E )2 ∫ 0π sin⁡ 𝜃 2 cos⁡ 𝜃 2 sin⁡4𝜃 2 d𝜃, (40)

the range 0 < 𝜃 ≤ π being the full span of deflections available (Fig. 5). The small-angle behaviour of the integrand controls the result:

σT ∼∫ 0δ d𝜃 (𝜃∕2)3 = 8∫ 0δd𝜃 𝜃3 = 8 𝜃−2 (−2) |0δ = +∞, (41)

4 The Hamiltonian and Hamilton’s equations of motion

Starting from

L = T − V = L(q1,…,qn,q˙1,…,q˙n,t), (43)

the Lagrange equations and the conjugate momenta are

0 = d dt ( ∂𝐿 ∂q˙i | qj,q˙j ) −∂𝐿 ∂qi | q˙j,qj,pi = ∂𝐿 ∂q˙i | qj,q˙j. (44)

Remark 1 (Midterm remark). In polar coordinates ∂𝐿∕∂𝜃˙ = p𝜃 = ℓz, where 𝜃 is the polar coordinate rather than the scattering angle of the preceding sections. What follows from it is struck out: L = L(r,ṙ,p𝜃), giving the Lagrange equation. The conserved momentum may not be substituted back into the Lagrangian and the Lagrange equations then reused.

4.1 The Legendre transformation

The transformation to be made is

q1,…,qn,q˙1,…,q˙n→q1,…,qn,p1,…,pn, (45)

and for a function of two variables it rests on

f(x,y)⟹df = ∂𝑓 ∂𝑥dx + ∂𝑓 ∂𝑦dy. (46)

4.1.1 The thermodynamic case

In this subsection T, V and p denote temperature, volume and pressure, not the kinetic energy, potential and momenta of the preceding sections. The first law, for a fluid at constant N, is

dU = ¯dQ + ¯dW = TdS − pdV,U = U(S,V )the internal energy. (47)

To have T and V as the variables instead, introduce the Helmholtz free energy F = A = U − 𝑇𝑆:

dA = dU −d(𝑇𝑆) = TdS − pdV − TdS − SdT = −SdT − pdV ⟹A = A(T,V ). (48)

4.1.2 The general case

The same transformation in the abstract, with u here the variable conjugate to x rather than the 1∕r of Eq. (19), reads

f(x,y) = udx + vdy,u = ∂𝑓 ∂𝑥,v = ∂𝑓 ∂𝑦,(x,y)→(u,y). (49)

Erratum. The left-hand side of Eq. (49) is written f(x,y) where the differential df is meant, as Eq. (46) has it and as Eq. (50) then consumes it.  Reproduced above as written.

g = f − 𝑢𝑥⟹dg = df −d(𝑢𝑥) = udx + vdy − udx − xdu = −xdu + vdy,g = g(u,y). (50)

4.2 The Hamiltonian

Applying this to L(q1,…,qn,q˙1,…,q˙n) with

pi = ∂𝐿 ∂q˙i | qj,q˙j (51)

gives

H = −(L −∑ ipiq˙i) , (52)

written this way to mirror g = f − 𝑢𝑥 of Eq. (50), with H = −g; it is the usual ∑ ⁡ ipiq˙i − L. Then

dH = −dL + ∑ id(piq˙i) = −∂𝐿 ∂𝑡 −∑ i ( ∂𝐿 ∂qidqi + ∂𝐿 ∂q˙idq˙i) + ∑ i (pidq˙i + q˙idpi) , (53)

so that

dH = −∂𝐿 ∂𝑡 + ∑ i (q˙idpi − ∂𝐿 ∂qidqi) ⟹H = H(p1,…,pn,q1,…,qn,t). (54)

Erratum. The first term of Eqs. (53) and (54) carries no differential: with every other term a differential, it is −∂𝐿 ∂𝑡 dt.  Reproduced above as written.

Reading off the coefficients gives the canonical equations of motion, the middle one using the Lagrange equation (44):

∂𝐻 ∂pi = q˙i,∂𝐻 ∂qi = −∂𝐿 ∂qi = −ṗi,∂𝐻 ∂𝑡 = −∂𝐿 ∂𝑡 (55)
